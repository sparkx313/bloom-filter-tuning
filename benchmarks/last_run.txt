3. what does a target false positive rate cost in bits
-------------------------------------------------------
  20000 items in a filter sized by the textbook formula, probed 20000 times.

    target   theory   actual k/target   k  predicted   measured   ratio      z
   0.20000   3.3498   3.3498   2.3219   2   0.202106   0.207450    1.03    1.9
   0.10000   4.7925   4.7926   3.3219   3   0.100712   0.101200    1.00    0.2
   0.03000   7.2984   7.2984   5.0589   5   0.030004   0.029600    0.99   -0.3
   0.01000   9.5851   9.5851   6.6439   7   0.010039   0.011050    1.10    1.4
   0.00300  12.0910  12.0910   8.3808   8   0.003011   0.002300    0.76   -1.8
   0.00100  14.3776  14.3776   9.9658  10   0.001000   0.000900    0.90   -0.4
   0.00010  19.1701  19.1701  13.2877  13   0.000100   0.000150    1.50    0.7
  `theory` is the closed form's bits a item, `actual` is what the integer
  ceiling of m costs, and `k/target` is the (m/n)ln2 the formula wanted.
  `ratio` is measured over predicted and `z` is that same gap in sampling
  sigma for 20000 probes: the widest ratio belongs to target 0.00010 at 1.50,
  which is 0.7 sigma -- the formula and the hash agree as well as 20000 probes
  can tell them apart. Tightening the target shrinks the number being measured
  and not the noise: at target 0.00010 the expected hit count is 2.0 out of 20000
  probes, so one extra hit moves `ratio` by 0.50 -- that row is a readout of
  the probe count, not of the formula.

  the shortcut -- k=7 in memory that was sized for a 5% target -- costs 15589
  bytes for 20000 keys and measures 0.06185 where 0.01000 was asked for. Choosing k
  harder does not rescue it: sweeping k over the same 15589 bytes bottoms out at
  0.04755 (k=4), still 4.8x the target, because m is what sets the floor.
  The filter actually sized for 1% costs 23963 bytes and measures 0.01105. Tuning
  k is a free few percent; shrinking m is a wall.

4. what a query costs, and what it saves
-----------------------------------------
  each structure holds the same 20000 keys; a probe batch of 2000 present and
  2000 absent keys is timed, so hit and miss are both measured and not
  assumed symmetric. Insertions are timed on a second,
  identical structure so they cannot inflate the row that reports them, and
  every timing is the best of five passes after two discarded warm-ups.

  structure       insert ns    hit ns   miss ns     bytes  bytes/key   false +
  Bloom               13379     17113     16204     23963        1.2      0.8%
  CountingBloom       25177     23415     20469     95851        4.8      0.7%
  DynamicBloom        15040     17901     42893     57265        2.9      0.6%
  python set             70       187       165   3486258      174.3      0.0%
  the set is exact -- no false positives, because it stores the keys -- and
  it is 145.5x the memory of the Bloom per key, strings included. That ratio is
  the whole argument for the filter, and the `false +` column is the price.
  CountingBloom pays 4.0x the bits of the Bloom because each slot is a nibble
  and answers a hit in 1.37x the Bloom's 17113 ns; DynamicBloom looked at 24
  slots per probe against the Bloom's 7, so its miss column is 2.65x at
  42893 ns against 16204. Read those as ratios, not absolutes: the identical
  batch of probes varies 81% pass to pass on this machine, so anything
  inside that is a tie. A thousand Bloom misses cost 16.2 ms at best of five,
  because every slot check is a Python-level call rather than a word compare.

5. does the cheap hashing scheme cost accuracy?
------------------------------------------------
  identical m, k and key set for all three; only the position generator
  changes. `independent` is the variant the closed form was derived for.

  scheme          k  predicted   measured       z  insert ns   probe ns
  double          7   0.010039   0.009200    -1.2      42700      14317
  splitmix        7   0.010039   0.009850    -0.3      41900      15701
  independent     7   0.010039   0.010800     1.1      55300      30571
  the widest gap from the closed form is -1.2 sigma (double): every scheme lands
  within sampling noise of the formula. What does separate them is price --
  `independent` costs 2.1x the cheapest scheme per probe (30571 ns against
  14317), so on this corpus the cheap schemes are not measurably worse and
  are measurably faster.

  position collisions inside one key are the one thing double hashing gives
  up: k draws from a shrinking pool instead of k independent ones. Sample of
  5000 keys, same m=191702 and k=7 for all three:
  double          0 of 5000 keys hash to fewer than 7 distinct slots
  splitmix        1 of 5000 keys hash to fewer than 7 distinct slots
  independent     1 of 5000 keys hash to fewer than 7 distinct slots
  the birthday arithmetic says 0.5 of the 5000 should collide at all, and
  the worst scheme here measured 1 -- 1.8x that estimate. Collisions are
  real but rare at this size, which is why the accuracy table above cannot
  tell the schemes apart and the timing table can.


6. deletion, saturation and the false negative it can create
-------------------------------------------------------------
  a counter is 4 bits, so a slot hit 15 times records nothing more while its
  removals keep subtracting. That is the only way a filter with `remove` can
  lose a key it still holds, so this section drives the filter hard enough to
  saturate it and reads the damage back out.
  Setup: 19171 counters with k=7, 200 sentinel keys live for the whole run, and
  rounds of `insert N fresh keys, delete those same N` for N at 1x, 5x and
  22.5x the design capacity. `sat+` is increments refused at 15 during the
  insert half of the round; `refused` is deletions that found a 0 and did
  nothing; `missing` counts sentinels gone after the delete.

  load x   round         N      sat+   refused   missing measured fp  predicted
     1.0       1      2000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000
     1.0       2      2000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000
     1.0       3      2000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000

     5.0       1     10000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000
     5.0       2     10000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000
     5.0       3     10000         0         0         0     0.00000    0.00000

    22.5       1     45000     46904     13222       175     0.14880    0.14780
    22.5       2     45000     82807     13165       168     0.15345    0.15349
    22.5       3     45000     91348     13255       169     0.16275    0.15832

  at design load nothing saturates: 0 increments refused, 0 deletions
  refused, 0 sentinels lost, 1354 counters left and a rate of 0.00000 against a
  predicted 0.00000. Deletion is exact there because every contribution was
  recorded before it was subtracted.
  five times the load is clean on the same counts (0, 0, 0 lost), so the
  cliff this section is looking for sits between those two, not at 1x.
  at 22.5x the same three rounds refuse 221059 increments and 39642 deletions over
  135000 churned keys, and the filter comes back holding 31 of its 200 sentinels
  -- 84.5% false negatives, which a plain Bloom cannot produce at all. The
  rate goes the other way: 0.16275 for the counter filter against 1.00000 for the
  bit array that still holds all 171200 keys it was ever given. So deletion does
  drain, and up to about the design load that drain is worth the 4x memory;
  past it the failure mode is silent loss of live keys, not a slower answer.

7. what not knowing the capacity costs
---------------------------------------
  the stream turns out to be 40000 keys. `first layer` below is the number the
  filter was given when it was built, and it grows a layer whenever a layer
  fills; `known` is the one filter sized for the true count at the end.

  first layer    layers     bytes  vs known     budget   measured  probes   wasted
  40000 known         1     47926       1.0x    0.01004    0.01085       7       0%
  20000 keys          2     89923       1.9x    0.00505    0.00540      16      33%
  10000 keys          3    114528       2.4x    0.00753    0.00875      24      43%
  4000 keys           4    107236       2.2x    0.00886    0.00945      32      33%
  4000 decay=1        4     71891       1.5x    0.02986    0.03200      28      33%
  starting at 4000 keys -- 10% of the true count -- ends at 4 layers, 107236 bytes
  against the known-size filter's 47926, so 2.2x the memory and 32 slots per
  probe against 7, while the measured rate (0.00945) still lands under the 1%
  that was asked for. `wasted` is the other half of the story: the last layer
  is paid for in full and used only in part, and that unused room is what
  makes the next layer affordable when it arrives.

  `decay` is the knob that sets each new layer's budget, and the last row is
  the same growth with decay=1.0 -- every layer handed the full 1.00%
  instead of a shrinking share: 4 layers and 71891 bytes against 107236, so 1.5x
  cheaper, at a measured 0.03200 against 0.00945 and
  a budget of 0.02986 against 0.00886. The bound column tracks the measurement on
  both rows, which is the point of `fp_bound`: the layer budgets only sum to
  the target when each layer is handed a smaller one than the last.
